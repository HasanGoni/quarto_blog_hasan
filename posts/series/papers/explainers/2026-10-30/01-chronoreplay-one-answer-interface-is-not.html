ChronoReplay: One Answer Interface Is Not Enough for Streaming Spatial Intelligence

arXiv 2610.34283 · picked for the week of 2026-10-30 · Addresses the critical issue of preserving metric spatial relations in VLMs, which is essential for accurate defect localization and segmentation in industrial X-ray and casting applications.

ChronoReplay is a training method for spatial vision-language models that prevents 'interface lock-in,' where focusing on one type of answer (like distance) hurts performance on others (like left/right). By replaying mixed spatial questions in their original chronological order and keeping answers in their native formats, a single model improves across categorical, metric, and temporal benchmarks simultaneously.

Explain it simply

Explain it like I'm five

Imagine you are playing a game where you have to remember where your toys are. If you only practice saying 'it is on the left,' you might forget how to say 'it is two steps away.' This paper shows that if you practice both types of questions at the same time, you get better at both. It is like learning to ride a bike and swim at the same time instead of just one. The trick is to remember the questions in the order they happened, like a story. This helps the computer brain keep all the details in one place. So, it can answer 'where is it?' and 'how far is it?' without getting confused.

Explain it like I'm a data scientist

The paper addresses a specific failure mode in post-training spatial VLMs called 'interface lock-in.' When models are trained to optimize for a single answer format (e.g., categorical left/right), they often degrade in performance on other spatial readouts (e.g., metric distance or temporal localization). This suggests that the shared representation is not preserving the complementary spatial distinctions required for heterogeneous queries, leading to uneven transfer across benchmarks like OVO-S, OST, and UCS.

ChronoReplay solves this by combining two components: Causal Replay construction and Family-Balanced Learning. First, it constructs training episodes that preserve the chronological order of observations, ensuring that every target (distance, direction, timestamp) is attached to its valid causal prefix. This prevents ambiguity arising from identical local displacements with different origins. Second, it uses a balanced objective function that normalizes the loss from 'preservation' (single-turn) and 'trajectory' (multi-turn) families, allowing the model to learn native answer formats without collapsing them into a single canonical type. This results in a unified spatial state that supports diverse readouts.

Building chronologically ordered episodes with native targets

The method constructs a replay pool where every target is kept in its native format (option, metric, timestamp). Episodes are ordered chronologically so that each target is attached to its legal causal prefix. This ensures that the model learns the relationship between the history and the answer, rather than isolated question-answer pairs.

Normalizing loss within preservation and trajectory families

The method calculates separate risks for the preservation family (single-turn examples) and the trajectory family (multi-turn episodes). It normalizes these risks to prevent one family from dominating the training due to differences in size or response length. This step prepares the data for balanced optimization.

Combining family risks with explicit weighting

The normalized risks are combined into a single objective function using a weight parameter alpha. In the main experiments, alpha is set to 0.5, giving trajectory loss one-third of the total mass and preservation loss two-thirds. This explicit control regulates the relative influence of each family on the shared model parameters.

Training the VLM on heterogeneous spatial supervision

The VLM is post-trained on the combined replay pool. The model learns to maintain a reusable spatial state that supports native readouts for categorical, metric, and temporal queries. The training process preserves the source-native targets, avoiding the need for answer-space transformations.

Selecting causal frames for test-time evidence

At inference time, an adaptive frame selection policy is applied to the trained model. This policy changes the evidence access by selecting specific frames, providing an additional gain in performance at a lower visual cost. This step is separate from the training process and can be applied to the same checkpoint.
