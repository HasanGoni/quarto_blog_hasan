The Surprising Effectiveness of Deleting Weights in LLM Post-Training

arXiv 2611.10866 · picked for the week of 2026-11-13 · Demonstrates that deleting a tiny fraction of weights can match full fine-tuning, offering a novel, low-cost post-training strategy for adapting small VLMs to specific defect detection domains.

arXiv HF paper page Code

The paper introduces Deletion Tuning, a method that acquires new capabilities in Large Language Models by zeroing out fewer than 0.05% of their weights without changing any other values. It demonstrates that this simple deletion strategy matches full fine-tuning performance at scale across reinforcement learning, supervised fine-tuning, and distillation regimes while using a significantly smaller adapter and forgetting far less prior knowledge.

Explain it simply

Explain it like I'm five

Imagine you have a huge, complicated puzzle that is already almost finished. Instead of adding new pieces or moving the old ones around, you just take away a tiny handful of pieces that are in the wrong spot. Surprisingly, the puzzle still works perfectly and even learns new tricks! This is much easier than trying to fix every single piece in the whole box. It also means you don't forget how the puzzle looked before you started. It is like cleaning up a messy room by throwing away just a few crumpled papers instead of rearranging all the furniture. The room looks better and you didn't have to move the heavy sofa.

Explain it like I'm a data scientist

The core problem is that standard post-training methods like full fine-tuning (FFT) or LoRA modify many parameters, leading to large model checkpoints and significant catastrophic forgetting of prior capabilities. Existing sparse methods often write new float values at selected positions, but the paper asks if simply deleting existing weights is sufficient to learn new tasks. The gap addressed is the lack of a post-training mechanism that is both extremely compact and preserves base model knowledge without complex regularization or value updates.

Deletion Tuning works by learning a binary mask over the gate projection weights of transformer blocks. During training, a trainable logit per weight is passed through a sigmoid function that is annealed to become a hard threshold. At deployment, weights with positive logits retain their original pretrained values, while those with negative logits are set to zero. This results in an adapter that is just a bitstream, making it orders of magnitude smaller than FFT checkpoints while maintaining accuracy comparable to full fine-tuning at large scales.

Initialize trainable logits using Wanda pruning scores

The method starts by computing Wanda scores for the gate projection weights, which estimate the importance of each weight. Trainable logits are then initialized from two Gaussians based on whether the weight is above or below a quantile of these scores. This provides a smart starting point for the mask, biasing it to keep important weights initially.

Train the mask using a smooth sigmoid relaxation

During training, the binary decision is relaxed using a sigmoid function applied to the logits. The sharpness of this sigmoid is annealed over time, starting smooth and becoming steeper. Gradients flow through this soft mask to update the logits, allowing the model to learn which weights to delete while maintaining differentiability.

Minimize the specific post-training objective with regularization

The model is trained on the specific task objective, such as GRPO for reinforcement learning or SFT for supervised learning. A sparse weight regularizer is added to the loss to mildly bias the mask toward retaining higher-magnitude weights. The base model weights remain frozen, and only the gate logits are updated.

Anneal the sigmoid sharpness to approach a hard threshold

As training progresses, the sharpness parameter alpha increases linearly. This pulls the soft mask closer to a hard step function, where the decision to keep or delete a weight becomes more binary. By the end of training, the soft mask and the hard mask agree closely, ensuring that the learned decisions are robust.

Apply the hard mask to zero out selected weights

At deployment, the soft mask is replaced by a hard mask based on the sign of the logits. Weights with positive logits retain their original pretrained values, while those with negative logits are set to zero. This results in a model that is identical to the base model except for the deleted weights, requiring no new parameter storage.

Compress the binary mask for efficient storage

The final adapter is the binary mask itself, which is bit-packed and compressed using zstd. Because the mask is highly redundant (fewer than 0.05% of weights are deleted), it compresses to a very small file size, often just a few megabytes. This makes it easy to store and switch between different task-specific adapters.
