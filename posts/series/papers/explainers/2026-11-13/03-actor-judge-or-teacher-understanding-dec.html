Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents

arXiv 2611.17167 · picked for the week of 2026-11-13 · Analyzes decision models as judges or teachers, directly applicable to the user's work on auto-label verification and judging in industrial defect detection pipelines.

This paper investigates whether decision models that perform poorly as agents can still serve as effective teachers for reinforcement learning. It demonstrates that while these models fail to act correctly on tasks with ordered subgoals, they can accurately judge action contributions when given hindsight and asked specific questions about progress, leading to significant improvements in policy success rates.

Explain it simply

Explain it like I'm five

Imagine you have a robot helper who is bad at cooking but great at watching you cook. When the robot tries to cook, it forgets to heat the soup before serving it. But when it watches your whole cooking video, it can point out exactly which steps helped make the soup good. The researchers taught a new robot by having the bad-cooking robot watch its videos and give scores. The new robot learned much better than if it only got a final grade. This works because watching the end result helps the judge see why early steps mattered. So, a bad doer can be a good teacher if it watches the whole story.

Explain it like I'm a data scientist

The core problem is that outcome-based rewards in reinforcement learning assign the same credit to every action in a trajectory, failing to distinguish between useful steps and no-ops, especially in long-horizon tasks with ordered subgoals. While decision models are often evaluated as actors, they frequently fail in this role by prioritizing easier subgoals over prerequisite ones, raising the question of whether their failure to act implies a failure to judge.

Jargon buster (each with a daily-life picture)

Decision Model
Like a multiple-choice quiz engine that gives a probability score for each option instead of writing an essay. A pretrained model that outputs a typed choice with probabilities for each option, used here to select actions or score them.
GRPO
A group grading system where students are compared to their peers to determine who improved the most. A reinforcement learning algorithm that uses group-relative advantages to update policies, here augmented with step-level scores.
Hindsight
Watching the entire movie before judging how important the first scene was. A context setting where the judge sees the full trajectory, including the outcome, allowing it to assess the contribution of earlier actions.
Progress Question
Asking 'Did this step move us closer to the goal?' rather than 'Will we win?'. A specific query asking if an action helped achieve the goal, which better distinguishes useful actions from no-ops than asking about task success.
Subgoal Dependency
You must heat the mug before placing it on the table; doing it in reverse order fails the task. A task structure where one requirement must be satisfied before another, which causes actor models to fail by prioritizing easier, independent subgoals.
Constrained Readout
Reading the raw confidence scores from the first word the model wants to say, rather than letting it write a full sentence. A method of extracting decision probabilities by using the first-token probabilities of valid answer labels from a general LLM.

Assessing decision models as actors on ALFWorld tasks

The models are queried to choose the next action in environments like ALFWorld. They consistently fail on tasks with ordered subgoals, such as heating a mug before placing it, because they are drawn to easier, independent subgoals. This establishes the baseline failure mode of the models when acting.

Configuring the context for judging recorded trajectories

The models are reconfigured as judges to score actions in recorded trajectories. Two contexts are tested: Prefix, which shows only the history up to the action, and Hindsight, which shows the entire trajectory. This setup allows the analysis of how much future information helps the model evaluate past actions.

Comparing questions about task success vs action contribution

The judges are asked two different questions: Value, which asks if the task will succeed after the action, and Progress, which asks if the action helped achieve the goal. The Progress question is found to better separate useful milestone actions from no-ops, especially when combined with Hindsight context.

Extracting probability scores for each action

For each action in the trajectory, the decision model returns a score in [0, 1] based on the chosen question and context. These scores reflect the model's assessment of the action's contribution to the goal, providing fine-grained supervision signals that are not available from outcome rewards alone.

Integrating teacher scores into GRPO advantages

The extracted scores are centered within each rollout group to remove bias and then added to the outcome advantage of GRPO. This step ensures that actions scored above the group mean receive a larger training signal, effectively using the decision model as a step-level critic.

Training the policy with step-level supervision

The policy is updated using the combined advantages from GRPO and the decision model's scores. This process allows the policy to learn from the nuanced feedback of the teacher, leading to higher success rates on tasks with ordered subgoals compared to training with outcome rewards alone.
